AutoHarmonizer

Real-time vocal harmonizer on a Teensy 4.1 custom PCB: an I2S MEMS mic captures your voice and pitch-shifts it up to ±12 semitones to sing a harmony with you.

Role
Solo, from schematic to DSP
Brain
Teensy 4.1
Audio
I2S MEMS mic in · Teensy Audio Shield out
Interface
OLED · rotary encoder
Power
9 V battery · switch · buck regulator

What it doesSing a duet with yourself

Sing into the microphone and pick an interval anywhere from 12 semitones down to 12 up with the rotary encoder and OLED. A copy of your own voice comes out shifted to that interval, singing the harmony with you in real time.

The display shows the note it hears you singing, the interval, and the harmony note it's producing. In the photo, an E6 shifted up 5 semitones becomes an A6.

DemoHear it harmonize

Full demo with sound: singing into the mic while turning the encoder through different harmony intervals.

Signal pathMic to harmony

An I2S MEMS microphone streams digital audio straight into the Teensy 4.1, which pitch-shifts it by the selected interval with a WSOLA algorithm I wrote myself. The result goes out through the Teensy Audio Shield. The OLED and encoder handle the interface, and a 9 V battery feeds the board through a switch and buck regulator.

Everything sits on a custom carrier board I designed, with the Teensy 4.1, audio shield, microphone, OLED and regulator plugged in as modules.

Hardest problemWriting WSOLA for real time

The problem

The simple way to change pitch, playing audio faster or slower, also changes its length. A harmony has to stay locked in time with the singer, so pitch had to move while timing stayed fixed.

The approach

I wrote WSOLA (waveform similarity overlap-add) from scratch. It stretches audio in time by overlapping short windowed segments, each placed where its waveform best matches the one before it, so the joins stay smooth. Resampling the stretched audio back to its original length shifts the pitch.

Why it was hard

Early versions sounded warbly. Getting a harmony that sounded like a natural second voice, not a wobbling copy, was the hardest part of the project.

The fix

The warble came from how segments faded into each other. Changing the shape of the window used to cross-fade each segment smoothed the joins and got rid of it.
