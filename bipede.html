BiPede

A two-wheeled self-balancing robot that sweeps a room on its own, maps it, and reports what's lying on the floor.

What it doesOne sweep, one report

Start a sweep and BiPede explores the room on its own, maps it, and comes back to where it started, recording what its camera sees along the way. Afterwards a laptop turns that recording into a report of floor hazards like shoes, bags, cables, boxes and bottles: a photo of each, what it is, and where it sits on the map.

Why this designBuilt to see what's on the floor

  • Floor-level view. At about 30 cm tall, the camera sees the floor the way a person only would by kneeling, including under furniture. That matters anywhere things on the floor are a risk: homes, especially for older people, restaurants and warehouses.
  • Sweep on demand. The battery can't support constant patrolling, so BiPede does one sweep when asked and then idles. There's no charging dock.
  • Two wheels, not four. The inverted-pendulum design keeps the robot narrow and lets the legs set the camera height. The cost is that it has to balance actively the whole time.

ArchitectureEach job runs where it fits

The Pico 2 keeps BiPede upright. The Pi 5 maps the room and decides where to drive. The heavy detection work waits until after the sweep and runs on a laptop, so the robot's battery goes to balancing and exploring.

MechanicalLegs that set the camera height

BiPede has a box chassis and two 2-DOF legs with wheels at the tips. Each leg uses a four-bar parallelogram linkage driven by a single STS3215 servo, which lets the legs raise or lower the camera. I designed it all in SolidWorks after evaluating several straight-line linkages, including Hoekens, Chebyshev and Peaucellier–Lipkin.

ElectricalOne board for power and I/O

A 6S LiPo feeds a custom 65×65 mm two-layer board I designed in EasyEDA and had made by JLCPCB. It splits power into a 12 V servo rail and a 5 V logic rail through Pololu regulators and feeds both ODrive Micro motor controllers. Ground pours with about 60 stitching vias keep the CAN traces over continuous ground.

The same board carries the Pico 2 and breaks out headers for the Pi 5 UART link, the servo bus, and the BNO085 IMU.

FirmwareBalancing at 100 Hz on a Pico 2

The Pico 2 runs the balance controller at about 100 Hz: a feedback-linearized pitch loop wrapped in an outer velocity and position loop. It reads a BNO085 IMU over I2C, drives both wheel motors through ODrive Micros over CAN at 500 kbps, and runs the leg servos over half-duplex UART, while taking drive commands from the Pi.

I brought up each bus in isolation, with loopback tests and live telemetry, before integrating them.

SoftwareMapping and exploring with ROS 2

The Raspberry Pi 5 runs ROS 2 Jazzy. RTAB-Map builds the map from the OAK-D Lite depth camera plus wheel odometry, Nav2 with Regulated Pure Pursuit drives the robot, and a custom frontier explorer I wrote keeps choosing the next unexplored area until the room is covered. The map shown here is one BiPede built.

Hazard detectionFrom recording to report

After the sweep, the saved keyframes run through YOLOE, an open-vocabulary detector, on a laptop. Each detection is checked against depth: is it on the floor, close enough, not furniture, and not on someone's feet. Detections that pass are placed on the map and merged across views into one object, so each hazard appears once in the report.

Below, every detection from the five staged sweeps is placed on the real room. Green circles are objects I placed, blue are real objects already in the room, and red are false alarms. The small dots inside each circle are the individual sweeps, which shows how consistently each object was found and located.

Hardest problemThe encoder that wouldn't calibrate

The problem

ODrive calibration kept failing with errors that pointed different ways: phase resistance, then phase inductance, then a pole-pair/CPR mismatch.

The investigation

Raising calibration current to 3 A fixed the resistance error, and I corrected the CPR settings, but the mismatch stayed even with the right motor parameters. That pointed away from configuration and toward the encoder signal itself.

The fix

The ODrive Micro's MA702 encoder needs a diametrically magnetized disc, and my motors shipped with a ring magnet. I swapped in a disc at the right standoff, checked the field strength, and the motors calibrated cleanly.

ResultBalancing through pushes

BiPede holds 0.4–0.7° RMS pitch error while driving and recovers from hand pushes in under 2.5 seconds. Every layer, from the linkage geometry to the exploration code, is my own design.
